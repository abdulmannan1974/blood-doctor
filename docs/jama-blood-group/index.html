01 · The case

Everyone reacted. One brother did not.

In 2013, a pregnant woman's plasma reacted with almost every donor red cell tested. It did not react with her own cells or her brother's.

The antibody looked like one against a high-prevalence antigen: a marker carried by nearly everyone. Standard reference tests could not identify it. The same problem remained when new samples arrived in 2020.

Why did the brother matter? Siblings can inherit the same rare variant from both parents. If both lack a common red cell marker, the patient's immune system may recognise that marker on other people's cells as foreign.

The simple clue

If an antibody reacts with almost everyone except the patient and a sibling, think about a rare inherited antigen-negative type. The sibling pattern does not name the antigen, but it tells you where to look.

Sequencing both siblings found the same rare change in F11R, the gene for junctional adhesion molecule-A, or JAM-A.

02 · The mechanism

One letter changes the surface badge.

JAM-A sits in the cell membrane. It has two parts outside the cell, called D1 and D2. The JIMI antigen depends on one amino acid on D2: arginine at position 215.

The patient and her brother each had two copies of F11R c.644G>A. This changes Arg215 to Gln215, so their cells lack JIMI. The protein is still there: a laboratory anti-JAM-A antibody detected JAM-A on their red cells, though at a lower level than on control cells.

Analogy

Think of JAM-A as a badge on the red cell. Most badges carry an “R” at position 215. This family's badges carry a “Q”. The badge remains, but the patient's antibody recognises the R version.

03 · The evidence

How did the team prove it?

Finding a rare gene change was a lead. The decisive question was whether the patient's antibody reacted with JAM-A because of the amino acid at position 215.

1
Serology pointed to an unknown antigen.

Her antibody reacted with a broad range of cells, including rare null cells, but not with her own or her brother's cells.

2
Sequencing found one candidate.

Both siblings shared a homozygous F11R variant that changes an exposed part of JAM-A.

3
The antibody distinguished R from Q.

Soluble Arg215 JAM-A soaked up her antibody, so it could no longer react with red cells. Soluble Gln215 JAM-A did not.

4
A control antibody checked the proteins.

A mouse anti-JAM-A antibody reacted with both versions. This showed that the Gln215 protein was present and still recognised by an antibody to JAM-A.

5
Cells gave the same answer.

Her antibody bound engineered cells carrying Arg215 JAM-A, but not cells carrying Gln215 JAM-A.

Same protein, one amino-acid difference: that contrast is the key proof.

The team also showed that the patient's antibody bound platelets in the laboratory. Washed platelets removed it from plasma, and antibody recovered from the platelets reacted again with control red cells.

04 · The reasoning trap

“Benign” did not mean “invisible”.

Computer tools rated p.Arg215Gln as unlikely to damage JAM-A function. That can be true and still miss the blood group. These tools ask whether a protein is likely to work differently. The immune system asks whether a surface detail looks different.

FRCPath / FCPS point

A variant can be functionally mild yet define an alloantigen. For a new blood group, check whether the changed residue is exposed on the cell surface, then test antibody binding directly.

The study did not directly test JAM-A function in the siblings. Their routine red cell and platelet counts were normal, but that does not prove all functions were unchanged.

05 · Bedside limits

What does this mean clinically?

Shown in this study

  • Anti-JIMI reacted with red cells carrying Arg215 JAM-A.
  • The antibody also bound platelets in laboratory experiments.
  • No haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn was reported in four live births from six pregnancies.
  • The patient and her brother had normal routine blood counts.

Still unknown

  • Whether anti-JIMI can cause a transfusion reaction.
  • How much fetal or newborn risk exists beyond this family.
  • Whether platelet binding causes FNAIT or platelet refractoriness.
  • How common JIMI-negative people are.

The paper reports a global variant allele frequency of 0.0000044 in gnomAD. This is a frequency for the gene variant. It is not an estimate of how many people are JIMI-negative.

Practical laboratory thinking

For an unresolved pan-reactive alloantibody, refer early to a specialist reference laboratory. If a compatible sibling exists, investigate that clue. For planned surgery or pregnancy, involve the blood service early so compatibility can be assessed and blood support planned.

These practical points are the author's general suggestions for rare antibodies, applied to JAMA. They are not management recommendations tested by the study.

06 · Quick recall

Test yourself

Try each question, then open its answer. These are short teaching versions of the questions in the supplied review.

1. Pan-reactive antibody; only the patient and her brother are compatible. Reference serology is unrevealing. What investigation could identify the cause?

Whole-exome sequencing of both siblings. Look for a shared rare homozygous variant in a red cell surface protein, then test whether the antibody binds it.

2. Why can a variant called “benign” by prediction software still define a blood group?

Function and antigen recognition are different questions. A surface change may be visible to an antibody without stopping the protein from working.

3. Which experiment most directly identified Arg215 as the target?

Paired inhibition. Arg215 JAM-A blocked the patient's antibody, Gln215 JAM-A did not, and a control anti-JAM-A antibody recognised both proteins.

4. Why did the team investigate platelets?

JAM-A is also found on platelets. The antibody bound platelets in vitro. Clinical platelet effects remain unproven.
